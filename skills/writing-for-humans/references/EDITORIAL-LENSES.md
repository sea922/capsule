# Editorial lenses

Use these lenses to find consequential writing problems without turning common
language or punctuation into a blacklist. Apply them in order. A later lens
must not damage a result already secured by an earlier one.

## 1. Truth and specificity

The most costly prose problem is not sounding synthetic. It is making the
reader believe something unsupported, vague, or more certain than the evidence.

Look for:

- praise without a stated basis: "powerful", "groundbreaking", "robust";
- vague attribution: "experts say", "research shows", "many believe";
- impressive-looking numbers or examples with no supplied source;
- abstract claims where the draft could name the actor, action, object, or
  consequence;
- confidence strengthened by editing: "may" becoming "will", or a correlation
  becoming a cause;
- a polished transition that hides a missing step in the reasoning.

Repair the evidence boundary before the wording. Name the source, qualify the
claim, supply the concrete fact, or remove the assertion. Preserve deliberate
hedging in technical, scientific, legal, medical, financial, and incident
writing.

## 2. Argument and information shape

Formulaic prose often reveals itself through structure before vocabulary.

Look for:

- a preamble that delays the answer or repeats the prompt;
- headings that divide a short thought into ceremonial sections;
- bullets used for fragments that form one argument, or paragraphs used for
  genuinely parallel choices or facts;
- a chat message or email packed into dense paragraphs, with the ask or
  several separate facts buried inside one block;
- rigid symmetry that gives minor points the same weight as the central one;
- a conclusion that merely repeats the introduction;
- transitions that announce movement instead of establishing a logical link;
- every section ending with a takeaway, recap, or motivational flourish.

Choose structure from the reader's job. Lead with the decision in operational
writing, the claim in argumentative writing, the event in narrative writing,
and the governing definition in reference material. Use headings and bullets
when they make relationships easier to see. Lay out chat messages and emails
with the message layout in `SKILL.md`.

## 3. Paragraphs and sentences

Good rhythm follows thought. Mechanical variation is another detectable house
style.

Look for clusters of:

- repeated sentence openings or subject-verb frames;
- sentences with several abstract nouns but no visible actor;
- nominalizations where a direct verb would reveal responsibility;
- throat-clearing clauses such as "it is worth noting that";
- negative pivots that manufacture drama instead of making a distinction;
- sets of three created for cadence rather than meaning;
- a clause followed by a colon and a restatement of the same clause;
- uniformly short punchy sentences or uniformly balanced long sentences;
- intensifiers that substitute for evidence.

Edit the cluster, not every instance. Keep repetition when it provides rhythm,
emphasis, parallelism, or terminological consistency. Keep technical nouns when
they are the domain's precise language.

## 4. Conversational and production residue

Remove traces of the writing process that do not belong in the artifact:

- greetings, acknowledgements, or offers to help that the genre did not ask
  for;
- references to prompts, instructions, token limits, knowledge cutoffs, or the
  assistant's process;
- canned openings such as announcing that the topic is important before saying
  why;
- canned closings that summarize, congratulate, or invite more questions when
  the document should simply end;
- placeholders, invented quotations, citation markers without sources, and
  unfinished notes to self.

Some of these forms are correct in chat, support email, or facilitation. Judge
them against the intended artifact rather than removing them universally.

## 5. Voice and genre

Voice is an authored pattern of choices, not a handful of quirks.

When samples are available, infer only traits that recur across representative
passages:

- level of formality and directness;
- typical sentence and paragraph movement;
- preference for examples, analogy, evidence, or narrative;
- characteristic vocabulary and terminology;
- stance toward the reader;
- restrained uses of humor, emphasis, fragments, or asides.

Separate stable voice from topic and genre. A casual personal post does not
license jokes in a legal notice. A technical sample does not prove the author
never uses first person elsewhere. When the evidence is thin or mixed, imitate
less and disclose the uncertainty.

Without samples, follow genre conventions and use a restrained voice. Do not
add opinions, biography, anecdotes, first person, messiness, or fake quotations
to simulate a human author.

## 6. Typography and local style

Typography is the lowest-priority lens because it is easy to count and easy to
overfit.

Review density and function rather than banning marks:

- dashes can mark interruption or emphasis but become mannered when every
  paragraph leans on them;
- colons can introduce a real elaboration but feel mechanical when they merely
  restate the preceding clause;
- parentheses can hold genuinely secondary information but should not carry the
  argument's main line;
- bold can support scanning but loses meaning when every paragraph contains a
  label;
- headings and bullets are useful when the content has real hierarchy or
  parallelism;
- contractions, fragments, and sentence-final prepositions depend on voice and
  genre, not a universal standard.

Apply the user's house style when supplied. Otherwise preserve typography that
is grammatically sound and useful.

## False-positive test

Before changing a suspected pattern, ask:

1. Does it make meaning, trust, rhythm, or reader effort worse here?
2. Is the problem the single instance, or only its repetition and density?
3. Does the genre or author's sample support it?
4. Would the proposed change preserve terminology and truth conditions?
5. Would a careful editor make this change without knowing the draft came from
   an AI system?

If the answer to the last question is no, leave it alone.
